Base paper explained

Elephants Never Forget: Memorization and Learning of Tabular Data in Large Language Models

Sebastian Bordt, Harsha Nori, Vanessa Rodrigues, Besmira Nushi, Rich Caruana
University of Tübingen & Microsoft Research · COLM 2024 · 31 pages

This is the paper our project is built on. It asks one question: when an LLM does well on a table, has it simply seen that table before?

In one minute

What this paper found

91%

GPT-4 could type out 125 of 136 Iris rows from memory. It can generate the whole Iris and Wine datasets.

−6 pts

Slightly changing the numbers in memorised datasets cut accuracy by about 6 points on average. On new datasets, the same change did nothing.

X1…

Hide the column names and scales and accuracy drops sharply, even on new data. LLMs lean on world knowledge.

< LR

Given only numbers, LLMs learn patterns worse than plain logistic regression, especially with many features.

Four take-home points. They memorise famous tables. Memorisation inflates their scores. Without names they lose a lot. And as pure pattern learners they are weaker than simple statistics.

Before we start

Few-shot learningGiving the model a handful of solved examples in the prompt, then asking about a new row.

Temperature 0The model always gives its single most likely answer, so runs are repeatable.
LR / GBTLogistic regression and gradient-boosted trees, the classical baselines.

Quick vocabulary so the rest makes sense. Contamination is the big one: it is like a student who has seen the exam paper in advance.

Motivation

Why ask this question?

  • LLMs are now used on tables for prediction, cleaning and generation.
  • But nobody checks whether the test table was in the training data.
  • The authors' warning sign: GPT-4 "predicts" stock prices from 2020 almost perfectly, but for 2022 (after its training data ends) it does worse than simply repeating yesterday's price.
Figure 1: Next-day prediction error of GPT-4 (lower is better). Redrawn from Table 1 of the paper.

This table opens the paper. In 2020 GPT-4's error on NASDAQ is 0.07 percent, which is suspiciously good. In 2022 it jumps to 1.30 percent, worse than the naive guess of yesterday's value at 1.20. It had simply seen the 2020 prices.

Study design

The study at a glance

The paper has four parts. First, a check: has the model seen the dataset? Second, does that matter for accuracy? Third, how good is the model when it has nothing but numbers? Fourth, a side result: the model can generate realistic rows of datasets it has seen.

Data

Two groups of datasets

Popular (online before 2021)

  • Iris (150 rows), Wine (178 rows), UCI
  • Titanic, Kaggle
  • Diabetes (Pima), OpenML
  • Adult Income, UCI
  • California Housing (20,640 rows)

Novel (2022+ or restricted)

  • ACS Income, ACS Travel: built from the 2022 US census survey
  • Spaceship Titanic: Kaggle 2022, synthetic
  • FICO: download only after signing an agreement
  • ICU: made by the authors, 102 patients

The novel group acts as the "clean" control: the models could not have seen these tables.

Six popular datasets that have been everywhere online for years, and five that the GPT models could not have seen. ACS Income was even designed to look like Adult, which makes it a neat comparison pair. Note: California Housing is used in the memorisation and sampling parts, not in the classification table.

Part 1 · Have they seen it?

Four memorisation tests

TestThe model is shownIt must writeWhy it proves memory
HeaderThe first few rows of the CSV fileThe next rows, exactlyChecks the start of the file, the part most copied online
Row completionA few consecutive rows from a random placeThe very next row, exactlyRandom rows can't be guessed
Feature completionOne whole row except a very unique value (a name, a long decimal)That missing value, exactlyUnique values can't be inferred, only recalled
First tokenA few consecutive rowsJust the first token of the next rowCompared statistically with guessing the most common value

If a test passes, that is definite proof of memory. If it fails, the data might still be in there, just not extractable. All tests run at temperature 0 and are released as the tabmemcheck Python package.

The logic is simple. Tables contain random measurements. You cannot reproduce random numbers digit for digit unless you have seen them. So a correct answer proves memory. But a wrong answer does not prove the opposite; the tests are strong in one direction only.

Part 1 · Results

Popular tables are memorised

Figure 3: Rows GPT-4 completed exactly in the row-completion test (Table 5 of the paper). Red = popular, blue = novel.
  • Header test passes on all popular datasets.
  • Titanic, Diabetes, Wine and Iris are memorised even at random rows, so essentially the whole file.
  • Adult and Housing: only the first rows.
  • Novel datasets: no evidence.
  • GPT-4 memorises a bit more than GPT-3.5.
  • Open models (OLMo 7B to Llama 3.1 405B) mostly memorise only the first rows, except Iris.

The pattern is very clear: either the model gets almost every row right, or almost none. GPT-4 completed 222 of 250 Titanic rows and 125 of 136 Iris rows, but zero on Adult and on all the new datasets. The one FICO hit was a row full of -9 values, which is easy to guess, so it is not counted as memory.

This is the cleverest part. Each format removes one more clue. Perturbed breaks exact memory. Task also renames columns and drops the dataset name, but keeps the meaning. Statistical removes the meaning too: X1 to X4 with standardised numbers. Logistic regression scores the same on all four (Table 9), so the problem itself doesn't change. Only the clues do.

Part 2 · Setup

How the LLM is asked

System: You are a helpful statistician, data scientist, and board-certified medical doctor with expertise in treating diabetes. You help to make predictions on the OpenML diabetes dataset. [...] User: IF Pregnancies = 5.0, Glucose = 136.0, BloodPressure = 82.0, [...], Age = 69.0, THEN Assistant: 0 [more examples] User: IF Pregnancies = 6.0, Glucose = 148.0, BloodPressure = 72.0, SkinThickness = 35.0, Insulin = 0.0, BMI = 33.6, DiabetesPedigreeFunction = 0.627, Age = 50.0, THEN Model: 1
Figure 5: Few-shot prompt, Diabetes, original format (Figure 12 of the paper).
  • Rows written as "Feature = Value".
  • 20 solved examples, picked to match the real label mix.
  • Temperature 0.
  • Compared with logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees.
  • 10 datasets × 4 formats × GPT-3.5 and GPT-4.

The model gets 20 solved rows and then a new one. It answers 0 or 1. Everything else is held constant; only the format changes.

Part 2 · Finding 1

Memory inflates the scores

Figure 6: Redrawn from Figure 2 of the paper; bar values computed from Table 4.
  • Memorised data: accuracy falls step by step as clues are removed. GPT-4 even beats logistic regression on the original format (+1.6), but not once the values are nudged.
  • Novel data: original, perturbed and task are flat. Only the statistical format hurts.

Look at the first panel: GPT-4 on memorised data beats logistic regression on 3 of 5 datasets in the original format. Nudge the numbers by 1 percent and the advantage disappears. On new data, nudging changes nothing. That gap is the effect of memory, roughly 6 points on average.

Part 2 · Finding 2

Column names do a lot of the work

Dataset (GPT-4)TaskStatisticalChange

Spaceship Titanic0.650.66+0.01
Table 1: GPT-4 accuracy, from Table 4 of the paper.
  • Going from "Task" to "Statistical" removes the names and natural scales, and accuracy drops everywhere.
  • This happens on new data too, so it's not memory. It's world knowledge about what "Age" or "Income" means.
  • The one exception, Spaceship Titanic, is about a fictional event, so its names carry no real-world meaning.

This is the second key effect: meaning. The model knows that high glucose suggests diabetes. Take the word "Glucose" away and it has to work from bare numbers, and it is much worse at that. Our project measures exactly this as the semantic effect.

Part 3 · Numbers only

As pure pattern learners, LLMs lag behind

GPT-3.5GPT-4Logistic regression1-nearest neighbourTabLLM (fine-tuned)
Figure 7: Synthetic task y = 1[xᵀz ≥ 0]. Values read from Figure 3 of the paper (approximate).
  • With 2 features, LLMs match 1-nearest-neighbour. With more features they fall fastest.
  • GPT-4 keeps improving with more examples; GPT-3.5 stays flat. Neither reaches logistic regression.

To check pure learning, the authors made a random task where the label depends on a hidden linear rule, with no names at all. LLMs do learn something, but they are less efficient than textbook methods. So their good scores on real new data mostly come from knowledge, not from learning the pattern in the examples.

Part 4 · Side result

LLMs can sample from data they've seen

  • GPT-3.5 was given only the name "California Housing" and its column names, plus example rows from other datasets.
  • It produced new rows whose map locations and correlations look like the real data.
  • Low temperature: rows bunch around the most common values. Higher temperature: more spread, sometimes off the map.

A side finding: the model has learned the dataset well enough to generate believable new rows. Mostly they are not copies, though GPT-4 copied about 10 percent. This matters for synthetic data: an LLM-made "synthetic" dataset may still leak the real one.

This matters for us because we use open models. The same drop appears when names are removed. But the authors warn that the "novel" datasets may have been released before these newer models were trained.

Honest view

Limitations

Stated by the authors

  • The training data of the models is unknown.
  • They couldn't insert datasets into training at random, which would be the ideal experiment.
  • Tests prove memory when they pass, but can't prove its absence.
  • Popular and novel datasets differ in other ways too.

Our observations

  • Only classification on complete data: no missing values, no imputation.
  • Only 5 + 5 datasets in the main comparison.
  • Main results use closed GPT models.
  • Memorisation is judged as yes/no, not as a score.

The authors are upfront about what they can't prove. On our side, the big gap is that they never look at missing values. That is where our project comes in.

Takeaway

The one sentence to remember

"Had we performed our evaluations without carefully controlling for memorization, we might have erroneously concluded that few-shot learning with GPT-4 frequently outperforms traditional statistical learning algorithms."

Bordt et al., Section 7

In short: before trusting an LLM's score on a table, check whether it has seen that table.
