Paper 1 · Hayat & Hasan · COLING 2025

CRILM: describe the gap, don't guess it

  • Instead of guessing a number, a big LLM (ChatGPT-3.5) writes a short phrase for each missing value, e.g. "Malic acid quantity missing for this wine sample."
  • Every row becomes sentences, with these phrases in the gaps.
  • A small model (Llama 2 7B or FLAN-T5) is fine-tuned on this text to predict the class.
Figure 1: Overview of CRILM (redrawn from Figure 1 of the paper).

CRILM sidesteps the hard part. It never estimates the missing value; it describes it in words and lets a fine-tuned model deal with it.

Paper 1 · Results

CRILM: what it found

Figure 2: Downstream accuracy (%) under MNAR with Llama 2 (Table 2 of the paper).

Found

  • Up to +10 points over the best of six imputers under MNAR; +13.6 with FLAN-T5 on Glass.
  • Feature-specific phrases beat plain "NaN".

Weak spots

  • Only famous UCI datasets, with no memorisation check.
  • No imputation error measured, only final accuracy.
For us: its MNAR rule (hide the lowest 30%) is one of our masks.

Big gains under MNAR, but on Iris-type datasets the LLM may know already. And since it never outputs a number, we can't say how close it got.

Paper 2 · Abate et al. · Pattern Recognition Letters 2026

MICE, with an LLM inside

  • Like MICE: fill the gaps in rounds, one column at a time.
  • But each prediction is an LLM call, with the record as text, the target written as "???" and 4 example patients (2 diabetic, 2 not, labels hidden).
  • Observed values are never changed. Stops when values stabilise (change < 0.01) or after 10 rounds.
Figure 3: The chained update loop (drawn from Section 3 of the paper; simplified).

This is MICE with the regression swapped for an LLM. Start from medians, then repeatedly ask the LLM for one value at a time, until nothing changes much.

Paper 2 · Results

Abate et al.: what it found

Figure 4: Downstream accuracy gain over MICE on Pima's real missing values (Table 1 of the paper, Italian prompt).

Found

  • Significantly better than MICE for 3 of 4 classifiers, but by only 1–2.5 points.
  • Under MNAR on DiaBD, plain median/mode was best on binary columns.

Weak spots

  • Credits gains to "clinical knowledge"; never checks whether Pima was memorised.
For us: source of our few-shot and step-by-step prompting, and of Pima's real missing pattern.

The most careful paper statistically, with permutation tests, Holm correction and effect sizes. The gains are real but small, and MNAR is where it wobbles.

Paper 3 · Li et al. · Findings of EMNLP 2025

AutoDCWorkflow: an agent writes the recipe

  • Input: a messy table plus a question to answer (the "purpose").
  • The LLM agent picks the columns that matter, writes a quality report for each, and chooses one of 6 OpenRefine operations (trim, upper, numeric, date, mass edit, regex).
  • It runs the operation, re-checks, and repeats until every column is clean.
Figure 5: Purpose-driven cleaning (redrawn from Figure 1 of the paper).

The output is not just a clean table but a reusable recipe of OpenRefine steps. The purpose decides which columns get cleaned.

Paper 3 · Results

AutoDCWorkflow: what it found

Figure 6: Answer F1 on 142 purposes (Table 7 of the paper). Uncleaned table: 0.22.

Found

  • Working step by step beats asking for the whole recipe at once, for every model.
  • Best: Gemma 2 27B, answer F1 0.63.

Weak spots

  • Small tables (10–100 rows), no missing-value filling, no memorisation check.
For us: same open models (Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 2 9B); its error types are our optional extension.

The main lesson is that agents working in steps clean far better than a single prompt. Errors were injected by rule: typos, extra spaces, case changes and text in number columns.

Paper 5 · He et al. · Findings of ACL 2025

LLM-Forest: ask several times, then vote

  • A graph links rows that share similar values, so similar rows can be found quickly.
  • Each "tree" gets its own set of similar rows and asks GPT-4o for the missing value plus a confidence level.
  • Answers are combined by a vote weighted by confidence: High 1.0, Medium 0.6, Low 0.3.
Figure 7: LLM-Forest pipeline (drawn from Section 3 of the paper).

Think of it as a random forest made of LLM calls. Each tree sees slightly different neighbours, so the vote smooths out one-off mistakes.

Paper 5 · Results

LLM-Forest: what it found

Figure 8: Imputation accuracy (%) on Diabetes by missing-data type (Table 5 of the paper).

Found

  • Best on 8 of 9 datasets, often by less than 1 point.
  • Biggest jump under MNAR: 51.3% vs about 41% for MICE and k-NN.

Weak spots

  • MAR/MNAR tested on one dataset only; prompts name the dataset; no memorisation check.
For us: exactly the kind of MNAR claim our fingerprint test checks.

Under MNAR the gap is about 10 points. The paper credits the LLM's external knowledge. We want to know whether that knowledge is understanding or memory.

Paper 6 · Zhang et al. · EMNLP 2024

Jellyfish: small local models, tuned for data work

  • Goal: avoid sending private data to GPT, so tune a model that runs on one local GPU.
  • Training mix: task examples, short knowledge hints ("the area code tells the city"), and reasoning written by Mixtral-8x7B.
  • Tuned with LoRA into Jellyfish-7B, 8B and 13B.
Figure 9: How Jellyfish is built (drawn from Section 3 of the paper). ED, DI, SM, EM = error detection, data imputation, schema matching, entity matching.

Three kinds of training data go in, and a base model comes out tuned for data preparation. It is then tested on tasks and datasets it was not tuned on.

Paper 6 · Results

Jellyfish: what it found

Figure 10: Average score over 19 datasets (Table 4 of the paper).

Found

  • The 13B model edges past GPT-4 on average (86.0 vs 84.2).
  • Knowledge hints matter a lot: one score rose from 7 to 59.

Weak spots

  • Says datasets are "unseen" by GPT but never tests it; tiny imputation test sets (65 and 86 rows).
For us: shows 4-bit-sized local models are good enough, and that hints (semantics) drive results.

The interesting part for us is the knowledge-hint result. Meaning matters hugely, which is exactly what our hidden-names condition removes.

Paper 7 · Qian et al. · MLSys 2024

UniDM: one recipe for many data tasks

  • Three automatic steps replace hand-written prompts.
  • Retrieve: the LLM picks useful columns, and the top 3 of 50 sampled rows.
  • Parse: rewrite "attribute: value" pairs as normal sentences.
  • Ask: turn the task into a fill-in-the-blank question.
Figure 11: UniDM's three steps, with the paper's own examples (Sections 4–5, Appendix B).

The parsing step is interesting: the authors say sentences look more like the model's training text, so the model is more likely to "hit relevant texts". That is basically triggering its memory.

Paper 7 · Results

UniDM: what it found

Figure 12: Imputation accuracy (%) on Restaurant (Table 1 of the paper).

Found

  • Reported the best results on imputation and error detection.
  • Choosing the right columns gave the biggest jump (82.6 → 90.7).

Weak spots

  • Built on a closed model (GPT-3); about 30–40× more tokens per query; no memorisation check.
For us: its biggest gain comes from column meaning, which our hidden-names version removes.

Smaller open models also did well here, around 86 on Restaurant for LLaMA2-7B and Qwen-7B.

Paper 8 · Wang et al. · PVLDB 2025

UnIMP: an LLM plus a graph network

  • Handles numbers, categories and text in one model.
  • Turns the table into a hypergraph: each cell is a node; each row and column connects its cells.
  • A frozen Llama2-7B turns each cell into features; small trained layers pass messages along rows and columns.
Figure 13: UnIMP pipeline (drawn from Section 3 of the paper). Pre-trained on all datasets, then fine-tuned per dataset (UnIMP-ft).

Each cell talks to its row and its column. The LLM is frozen; only the small graph and fusion layers are trained. That is why it is much stronger on numbers than plain prompting.

Paper 8 · Results

UnIMP: what it found

Figure 14: RMSE on Parkinsons, 20% MCAR, lower is better (Table 4 of the paper).

Found

  • About 28% lower error than MICE on average.
  • Plain LLM prompting (DFMs, Jellyfish) was worse than MICE on numbers, and timed out on big tables.

Weak spots

  • No repeated runs or significance tests; no memorisation check.
For us: proof that LLMs don't always win on numbers, so classical baselines are essential.

The key line for us: prompting an LLM directly gave higher error than MICE on Parkinsons and Zoo. LLM advantage is not universal.

Paper 9 · Ni et al. · IEEE ICDE 2025

ZeroED: find errors with almost no labels

  • Goal: spot wrong cells (typos, bad formats, outliers, rule breaks, missing values) with no human labels.
  • The LLM labels only about 5% of cells, picked as cluster representatives.
  • A small neural network learns from those labels and checks every cell.
Figure 15: ZeroED pipeline (drawn from Section III of the paper).

The LLM is used sparingly: to write checks and guidelines and to label a small sample. A cheap classifier does the bulk of the work, which saves a lot of tokens.

Paper 9 · Results

ZeroED: what it found

Figure 16: Error-detection F1 (Table III of the paper). FM_ED = asking the LLM row by row.

Found

  • Beats plain prompting on all six datasets, using over 90% fewer tokens on the largest one.
  • Even Llama3.1-8B did well.

Weak spots

  • Uses decade-old public benchmarks and credits "background knowledge"; no memorisation check.
For us: same open model families and sizes we plan to use.

Hospital, Flights and Beers are among the most-used cleaning benchmarks, so they are good candidates for memorisation testing in the future.

None of them checks whether the LLM had already seen the test data. That is the gap our project fills.

The last column is the one that matters. Every paper reports good LLM results on public data, and none of them rules out memorisation.
