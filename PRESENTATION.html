Our project asks a simple question with practical consequences. When a Large Language Model cleans a table better than a classical method, is that because it understands what the columns mean, or because it has already seen that exact dataset during training? We propose a controlled benchmark to separate these two explanations.

Introduction: Missing Data and Data Cleaning

  • Missing and erroneous values are among the most common defects in real-world tabular data.
  • The cleaning technique chosen affects every downstream analysis and machine-learning model.
  • Classical imputers (mean/median, k-NN, MICE, MissForest) estimate values purely from the statistical structure of the observed data.

Rubin's missingness mechanisms

MechanismProbability of a value being missing depends onImplication
MCARNothing (completely random)Easiest; most methods unbiased
MAROther observed attributesRecoverable from observed data
MNARThe unobserved value itselfMost realistic and most difficult

Data preparation takes up most of a data scientist's effort, and much of it is spent on missing and wrong values.

Rubin's taxonomy explains why the mechanism matters. Under MCAR the missingness is pure chance. Under MAR it depends on something we can see, for example older patients skipping a test. Under MNAR it depends on the hidden value itself, for example people with very high incomes not reporting income. A method that is unbiased under MCAR can become systematically biased under MNAR.

Classical imputers such as MICE and MissForest rely only on statistical relationships; they have no notion of what a column means.

Introduction: LLMs as a New Data-Cleaning Paradigm

  • A table row is serialised into text; a pre-trained LLM infers or repairs values using context and world knowledge.
  • Reported successes: imputation [1, 2, 5, 8], cleaning workflows [3], preprocessing [6, 7], error detection [9].
  • Most results come from widely circulated public datasets such as Iris, Wine, Pima, Adult, Hospital and Restaurant.
  • Popular LLMs have memorised many such tabular datasets verbatim [4].
Key question: does the LLM advantage come from understanding the data (semantic knowledge) or from recalling it (memorisation)?

The answer decides whether published results transfer to private, never-published data such as hospital, financial and enterprise records.

LLMs read a row as text. For example: "Glucose is 148, BMI is 33.6, Age is 50, Insulin is ???". They fill the gap using general knowledge of what these columns mean. Several recent papers report this works well.

There is a catch. Almost all of these results come from famous public datasets. Bordt et al. showed that GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 can reproduce rows of datasets like Iris, Wine, Titanic and Pima word for word; GPT-4 can generate the entire Iris dataset.

So when an LLM fills a missing value correctly on Pima, we cannot tell whether it reasoned or simply remembered. If it is memorisation, the advantage will not carry over to private data, which is exactly where cleaning is needed most.

Problem Statement

Existing situation: LLM-based cleaning is reported to outperform classical imputers [1, 2, 5].
↓
Limitation: evaluation uses famous public datasets with synthetic corruption, and the LLM may have memorised these datasets [4].
↓
Research need: a design that separates the semantic and memorisation sources of the LLM advantage, under identical corruption with exact ground truth.
↓
Objective: build and run a controlled benchmark that varies data origin and semantic context independently.
How much of the LLM advantage in data cleaning is semantic understanding, and how much is recall of memorised records?

Our problem in one sentence: how much of the measured advantage of LLM-based cleaning over classical methods comes from genuine semantic understanding, and how much from recall of memorised records?

A recent large-scale study by Mangussi et al., cited in our synopsis, found that LLM imputers beat MICE on real public datasets but lose to MICE on synthetic ones. Their synthetic datasets differed in two ways at once: no meaningful column names, and nothing that could have been memorised. The gap is consistent with both explanations. Our design changes one factor at a time.

Problem Statement: Research Questions

RQQuestion
RQ1How much of the LLM advantage on real data is due to semantic context, and how much to memorised records?
RQ2Does a dataset's measured memorisation score predict the LLM advantage on that dataset?
RQ3Do LLMs show unusual robustness to MNAR on memorised data that disappears on synthetic twins?
RQ4 (secondary)Does a synthetic twin reproduce the ranking of cleaning techniques found on the real data?
RQ5 (secondary)Do simulated missingness mechanisms leak the hidden values and inflate missingness-aware methods?

RQ1 is the main decomposition. RQ2 checks whether a measured memorisation score explains the LLM advantage. RQ3 proposes a behavioural test, the MNAR fingerprint, explained later.

RQ4 and RQ5 are secondary. RQ4 asks whether synthetic twins can stand in for private data when benchmarking. RQ5 asks whether simulated MNAR masks leak information.

The first three papers. CRILM does not estimate a number; it writes a phrase such as "malic acid quantity missing" and fine-tunes a small model on it. It reports up to 10 points improvement under MNAR, but only on classic UCI datasets.

Abate et al. replace each regression step of MICE with an LLM call that changes only the missing value. They use Pima's natural missingness and strict statistics. Gains over MICE are significant for three of four classifiers but small, about 1 to 2.5 points, and MNAR results are less consistent.

AutoDCWorkflow uses open models of 7–27B to generate OpenRefine cleaning workflows. Iterative reasoning beats direct prompting.

Bordt et al. is the key paper for our motivation. They built tests that ask a model to continue a CSV file or recover a unique value. GPT-4 reproduced 125 of 136 Iris rows. When memorised data is slightly perturbed, accuracy drops by about 6 points; on novel datasets nothing changes. Their tool, tabmemcheck, is what we use.

LLM-Forest combines several few-shot LLM answers by confidence-weighted voting. Its biggest claimed gain is under MNAR, which our fingerprint analysis will test.

UniDM deliberately rewrites table data into natural sentences to improve the "probability of hitting relevant texts" from the training corpus. In other words, it is designed to exploit pre-training knowledge, but never checks for memorisation.

UnIMP is important because it shows that purely prompt-based LLM imputers are worse than MICE on numerical data, for example on the Parkinsons and Zoo datasets. The LLM advantage is not universal.

ZeroED detects errors with LLM-generated rules and labels. Even an 8B model performs well within that pipeline.

Literature Review: Key Findings

Strengths of existing work

  • LLMs handle mixed numeric, categorical and text data [6, 7, 8]
  • Open 7–9B models are already competitive [2, 3, 6, 9]
  • Iterative, structured prompting beats single-shot prompting [2, 3]
  • Semantic cues (feature names, natural scales) strongly drive accuracy [4, 6]
  • Memorised datasets inflate few-shot accuracy [4]

Common limitations

  • 8 of 9 papers run no contamination check on their evaluation data
  • Gains are attributed to "prior" or "domain knowledge" without testing it [1, 2, 3, 5, 9]
  • Column names are never varied in imputation studies
  • MNAR is rule-based; findings conflict [1, 5] vs [2]
  • Small datasets and few repeated runs [1, 3, 5]

The literature shows LLMs are a real and useful addition to data cleaning, and even small open models do well. That matters for us because we run models locally.

Two findings point directly at our question. Bordt et al. show that replacing feature names with X1, X2 and standardising values cuts accuracy sharply. Jellyfish shows that injecting dataset-specific knowledge raised one F1 score from 7.14 to 59.29.

The most important limitation: eight of nine papers never check whether their evaluation datasets were in the LLM's training data, yet several explicitly credit "prior knowledge". The findings on MNAR also conflict: CRILM and LLM-Forest report the biggest gains there, while Abate et al., with stricter statistics, find MNAR results inconsistent.

Research Gap

Existing researchLLM imputers and cleaners outperform classical methods on public benchmarks (Iris, Wine, Pima, Restaurant, Hospital, Adult) [1–3, 5–9]
→
Observed limitationsLLMs memorise such datasets [4]; no cleaning paper tests for contamination or uses a semantics-preserving control; column names are never varied
→
Research gapNo controlled study separates the semantic and memorisation components of the LLM advantage under MCAR, MAR and MNAR with exact ground truth
→
MotivationPair real data with a perturbed-real variant and a synthetic twin; vary column names; apply identical masks; compare against classical imputers
Bordt et al. [4] isolate memorisation only for classification. No work does this for imputation or for missingness mechanisms.

This gap comes directly from the nine papers. On one side, eight papers report LLM success on public benchmarks. On the other side, Bordt et al. prove that LLMs memorise exactly these kinds of datasets and that memorisation inflates performance. The imputation and cleaning papers never connect these two facts.

Our approach fills the gap by construction. A synthetic twin keeps the column meanings and statistical relationships but contains no record the model could have memorised. Renaming columns removes the meaning but keeps the values. Varying the two independently lets us attribute the LLM advantage to each source.

Each dataset is first prepared as a complete reference table, so every repaired value can be checked against its true value.

We keep datasets from previous work separate from our own. The literature relies heavily on long-public benchmarks; only Bordt et al. deliberately compare popular and novel datasets.

Our high-exposure group contains famous datasets the models have very probably seen. Iris has 150 rows, Wine 178, Breast Cancer 569, and Pima 768 records with 8 attributes. The low-exposure group includes DiaBD, a 2025 Bangladeshi diabetes dataset with 5,288 records, plus one or two more released after the models' training cut-off, still to be chosen.

Pima's impossible zeros in glucose, blood pressure, skin thickness, insulin and BMI are real missing values. We use these real missingness patterns as realistic masks.

Dataset: Controlled Conditions and Corruption

Data origin ↓  /  Column names →Meaningful (with units)Anonymised (f1, f2, …)Misleading (permuted)

Twin fidelity checks

  • Marginal distribution distances
  • Correlation-matrix differences
  • Real-vs-synthetic detection classifier
  • Distance to closest record (no near-copies)

Corruption with exact ground truth

  • MCAR, MAR, MNAR (percentile rule and probabilistic self-masking)
  • Multivariate amputation; transplanted Pima masks
  • Rates of 10, 20 and 30%, over several seeds
  • The same mask is applied to every variant

This is the heart of the design: every dataset becomes up to nine controlled versions.

On the origin axis, the perturbed-real table has changed units, small noise and shuffled rows, so it keeps the true structure but no longer matches any memorised text. The synthetic twin, from the Synthetic Data Vault's Gaussian copula, has the same columns and joint distribution, but every row is new.

On the semantic axis, names are meaningful, anonymised, or deliberately misleading. Prompt templates keep the same length and structure, so formatting cannot explain differences.

The crucial rule is that the identical mask is applied to all versions of a dataset, so any performance difference can only come from the condition. Rule-injected value errors following AutoDCWorkflow are an optional extension.

Proposed Algorithm

Phase 1, curation and memorisation profiling: build complete reference tables; score each dataset and model with header, row- and feature-completion tests (tabmemcheck [4]).
↓
Phase 2, controlled conditions: real, perturbed-real and synthetic-twin variants × meaningful, anonymised and misleading names; verify twin fidelity.
↓
Phase 3, controlled corruption: MCAR, MAR, MNAR and transplanted masks at 10–30%; identical mask on all variants.
↓
Phase 4, cleaning: classical (mean/median, k-NN, MICE, MissForest) vs 4-bit LLMs (Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen2.5 3B/7B/14B, Gemma 2 9B); zero-shot, few-shot and iterative chained prompting [2].
↓
Phase 5, evaluation and decomposition: repair error, downstream accuracy, semantic and memorisation effects, MNAR fingerprint, statistical tests.

The algorithm is one reproducible pipeline in five phases; each phase produces an artifact the next one uses.

Phase 1 measures how much each model has memorised each dataset, using the checker released by Bordt et al.: the model is asked to continue the CSV header, complete the next row, or recover a unique value. This gives a memorisation score per dataset and model.

Phase 4 runs all cleaning techniques. The LLMs are open-weight models served locally in 4-bit precision. We test zero-shot, few-shot with four complete reference rows, and the iterative chained procedure of Abate et al. on a reduced subset. Generation uses temperature zero and a fixed output format; we record invalid or hallucinated outputs.

Proposed Algorithm: Key Formulations

E = repair error (e.g. NRMSE); LLM = language-model imputer; CL = classical imputer.

Memorisation effect (difference-in-differences)

Δmem = [ ELLM(twin) − ELLM(real) ] − [ ECL(twin) − ECL(real) ]

Semantic effect

Δsem = [ ELLM(anonymised) − ELLM(meaningful) ] − [ ECL(anonymised) − ECL(meaningful) ]

MNAR memorisation fingerprint

Dm = Em(MNAR) − Em(MCAR)   compared for LLM vs CL, on real data vs twin

A true inference method must degrade under MNAR; a model recalling memorised records need not.

The memorisation effect compares how much worse the LLM gets from real data to its twin, minus how much worse classical methods get. Classical methods cannot memorise, so their change captures any difference in difficulty; what remains is due to the LLM having seen the real data. This is difference-in-differences.

The semantic effect works the same way on the column-name axis.

The MNAR fingerprint is our distinctive diagnostic. Under MNAR the hidden values are systematically different from the observed ones, so an honest inference method must get worse. A model that remembers the record does not. If an LLM is unusually robust to MNAR on a famous dataset, and that robustness vanishes on the twin, that is behavioural evidence of memorisation.

The exact NRMSE normalisation and the regression for RQ2 are to be finalised during implementation.

Proposed Architecture

1. Dataset registryRaw data → complete tables; memorisation score per model
→
2. Condition generatorPerturbation, SDV synthesis, column renaming, fidelity checks
→
3. Mask generatorMCAR / MAR / MNAR, amputation, transplant; one shared mask
→
4. Imputer banksClassical bank ‖ LLM bank (fixed templates, constrained output, parser)
→
5. EvaluationError vs ground truth, downstream models, DiD, fingerprint, tests
Information flowArtifact passed to the next stage
Registry → GeneratorComplete tables annotated with exposure group and memorisation score
Generator → MasksUp to 9 verified variants per dataset
Masks → ImputersMasked tables + exact ground truth (same mask across variants)
Imputers → EvaluationImputed tables + logs (valid outputs, model calls, tokens, time)

The architecture has five modules connected by stored artifacts, which makes every stage reproducible and restartable.

The mask generator produces one mask per configuration and applies it to every variant; this is what makes the comparison fair. The two imputer banks run in parallel. On the LLM side, each masked row is serialised into a fixed template, and a tolerant parser extracts the answer. We log invalid or hallucinated outputs, model calls, tokens and time, so we can report accuracy together with cost.

Performance Evaluation: Metrics

LevelMetric
Repair qualityNRMSE and MAE (numerical); accuracy (categorical)
DistributionDistance between imputed and true value distributions
Downstream utilityAccuracy of random forest, gradient boosting, logistic regression
Reliability and costValid-output rate, hallucinated values, model calls, tokens, time
DecompositionΔsem, Δmem (difference-in-differences); MNAR degradation D
Ranking fidelity (RQ4)Kendall's τ and top-1 agreement, real vs twin
Leakage (RQ5)Gain in predicting a hidden value's range from the missingness pattern
NRMSE = √( (1/n) Σ (x̂i − xi)² ) / sx     MAE = (1/n) Σ |x̂i − xi|    (over masked cells only)

Because we create the missing values ourselves, we know every true value and can measure repair quality directly, not only through a downstream task.

For numbers we use NRMSE and MAE over the masked cells, and accuracy for categories. We also check whether imputed values follow the true distribution, because always predicting the mean can look acceptable on error but destroys the distribution.

Precision, recall and F1 are classification or detection metrics, used for example in ZeroED. Our core task is value repair, so our synopsis specifies NRMSE, MAE and accuracy instead.

Performance Evaluation: Protocol and Status

Planned evaluation protocol

  • Baselines: mean, median, k-NN, MICE, MissForest
  • LLMs: 5 open models × 3 prompting strategies
  • Repetition: several random seeds per configuration
  • Significance: paired permutation tests
  • Multiple comparisons: Holm–Bonferroni
  • Effect size: Hedges' g
  • Uncertainty: bootstrap confidence intervals

We have not yet produced experimental results; this presentation covers the research design.

The statistical protocol is deliberately strict, similar to Abate et al. Methods are compared on the same masks, so tests are paired. Because we run many comparisons, we correct with Holm–Bonferroni. Hedges' g tells us whether an effect is large enough to matter, and bootstrap intervals give the uncertainty. Classical methods act as the control in every difference-in-differences estimate.

Conclusion

Problem

  • Reported LLM advantages in data cleaning come from public datasets that LLMs are known to memorise; whether gains reflect understanding or recall is unresolved.

Proposed solution

  • A controlled benchmark that varies data origin and column semantics independently, with identical masks, classical controls and a difference-in-differences decomposition.

Expected contribution

  • Estimates of the semantic and memorisation components of the LLM advantage, and an MNAR memorisation fingerprint
  • Evidence on synthetic twins as privacy-preserving benchmarking proxies, and on MNAR leakage
  • An open-source benchmark harness and a contamination-aware evaluation checklist

LLM-based data cleaning looks promising, but the evidence comes from datasets the models have probably seen. Our benchmark separates the two possible explanations by construction.

The answer is useful either way. If the advantage is mostly semantic, practitioners can trust LLM cleaning on private data. If it is mostly memorisation, published benchmarks overstate its value. We make no claims about results yet; these are the expected contributions of the planned study.
